# How to Clear Your Criminal Record in Texas: Expunction vs. Sealing, Explained

Here's a fact that surprises almost everyone: in Texas, **a dismissed charge
doesn't disappear**. Neither does an arrest that never led to charges, or a
case you won at trial. The arrest record stays in state and federal databases
— and shows up on background checks for jobs, apartments, and licenses —
until a court orders otherwise.

The good news: Texas law provides two powerful tools for qualifying cases, and
recent years have expanded who qualifies.

## Tool #1: Expunction (destruction)

An expunction order requires agencies — the Department of Public Safety, the
arresting police department, the county, even the FBI's databases — to
**destroy or return** the records of a qualifying arrest. After expunction,
the law generally permits you to **deny the arrest ever happened**, including
on most job applications.

Expunction is generally available for:

- **Acquittals** — you went to trial and won
- **Dismissed charges** — depending on the reason for dismissal and, in some
  cases, a waiting period
- **Arrests with no charges filed**, after waiting periods that vary with the
  seriousness of the alleged offense (months for the lowest-level offenses,
  longer for felonies)
- **Completed pretrial diversion** programs, and certain veterans-court and
  mental-health-court programs
- **Pardons** and certain identity-theft situations (someone else used your
  name)

What generally *defeats* expunction: a conviction from the arrest, or
court-ordered probation on the charge (with narrow exceptions).

## Tool #2: Order of nondisclosure (sealing)

If you successfully completed **deferred adjudication** — a very common Texas
outcome where the case is dismissed after a supervision period — expunction
usually isn't available, but **sealing** often is. An order of nondisclosure
prohibits public disclosure of the records: private background-check companies
and most employers can't see them. Government agencies and certain licensing
bodies still can.

Eligibility depends on the offense (some, like family violence, are excluded),
your record since, and waiting periods that range from immediate to several
years. Certain one-time misdemeanor convictions — including, since 2017, some
first-time DWIs — can also qualify.

## Why the analysis is arrest-by-arrest

Record clearing in Texas is not a single application covering your whole
history. **Each arrest is analyzed separately** under the statute: its
disposition, its dates, its waiting period, and whether anything about it
disqualifies relief. It's common for someone to have one arrest that qualifies
for expunction, another that qualifies for sealing, and a third that qualifies
for nothing — yet.

That last word matters: many "no" answers are really "not until [date]."
Waiting periods run out. Knowing your dates means you can file the day you
become eligible.

## What the process looks like

1. **Verify the record.** Memory is unreliable; official records control. A
   proper screening pulls your actual criminal history first.
2. **File a verified petition** in the proper court, listing every agency
   that holds records — miss an agency, and its copy survives.
3. **The hearing.** Most properly-screened petitions are unopposed; many
   counties handle them briefly or on paperwork.
4. **The cleanup.** The signed order goes to every listed agency, and private
   background-check vendors get updated.

Realistic timeline for an uncontested case: **one to three months** from
filing to signed order, depending on the county.
