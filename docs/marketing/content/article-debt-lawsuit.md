# Sued by a Debt Collector in Texas? Here's What Happens If You Do Nothing

Every week, thousands of Texans open their door to a process server holding a
lawsuit from a company they've never heard of — Midland Credit Management,
Portfolio Recovery Associates, LVNV Funding, Jefferson Capital. The papers
look official because they are. And the most common response is the most
expensive one: doing nothing.

## The default judgment machine

Debt-buying companies purchase old accounts from banks and card issuers in
bulk, often for pennies on the dollar, then file lawsuits by the thousands.
Their business model doesn't depend on winning trials. It depends on
**defaults** — cases where the person served never responds, so the court
grants judgment automatically.

Once a default judgment exists, the debt buyer has powers it never had before:
it can seek to freeze non-exempt funds in your bank account, and the judgment
collects interest and can stay enforceable for a decade — renewable after
that. A debt you might have disputed, settled for a fraction, or defeated
entirely becomes a court-ordered obligation.

## The deadline is shorter than you think

Most consumer collection suits in Texas are filed in **justice court**, which
handles claims up to $20,000. In justice court, your written answer is
generally due by **the end of the 14th day after you were served**. In county
or district court, the deadline is calculated differently but is still
measured in days, not months. The clock starts when you're served — not when
you feel ready.

## What changes when you answer

Filing an answer does one deceptively simple thing: it forces the debt buyer
to **prove its case**. That means proving:

1. **It owns your debt.** The account may have been sold two, three, or four
   times. Each transfer needs paperwork that actually identifies *your*
   account — not just a spreadsheet reference.
2. **The amount is right.** Charge-off balances, post-charge-off interest, and
   fees all need support.
3. **The records are admissible.** Account records are hearsay unless a
   qualified witness lays the proper foundation — and a debt buyer's employee
   often can't speak to the original bank's record-keeping.

Debt buyers price their lawsuits assuming no one will make them do this. When
someone does, many cases are dismissed or settle for a fraction of the amount
claimed. No outcome is ever guaranteed — but the difference between answering
and not answering is the difference between having leverage and having none.

## Texas gives debtors real protections

Texas is one of the most protective states in the country for consumers:

- **Wages generally can't be garnished** for consumer debts like credit cards.
- **Your homestead** is protected from most creditors.
- Old debts are subject to a **four-year statute of limitations** — and both
  Texas and federal law restrict suing or threatening suit on time-barred
  debt.

None of these protections apply themselves automatically. They matter when
someone raises them.

## What to do this week if you've been served

1. **Note the date you were served.** Your deadline runs from it.
2. **Don't call the collector's law firm to "work it out"** before
   understanding your position — and be careful about acknowledging old debts.
3. **Gather what you have**: the citation and petition, any old statements,
   and your best memory of when you last paid.
4. **Get a case review.** Flat-fee defense means you know the total cost
   before you decide — often less than people expect, and far less than a
   default judgment.
