# Defenses, Counterclaims, and Settlement Posture

## Denials — what goes in every answer

- **General denial** (TRCP 92; TRCP 502.6 in JP court). Puts every element in issue.
- **Verified denials** (TRCP 93), sworn by the client (or unsworn declaration under
  Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 132.001). Select by facts:
  - **93(10) — denial of account**: mandatory whenever the petition is verified as a
    sworn account under TRCP 185; without it the account is prima facie proved.
    Credit-card and debt-buyer suits are generally not proper Rule 185 sworn accounts
    under Texas case law (no "systematic record of goods sold" between the parties),
    but deny under oath anyway rather than litigating the characterization.
  - **93(8) — denial of the genuineness of an assignment or endorsement**: use against
    every debt buyer; forces proof of each link in the chain of title.
  - **93(1)–(2) — capacity/standing**: plaintiff lacks legal capacity to sue or is not
    entitled to recover in the capacity in which it sues. Pair with 93(8) for
    assignees.
  - **93(9) — usury denial** only if interest facts support it (rare on bank cards due
    to federal preemption/exportation; consider for payday, RTO, and private lenders).

## Affirmative defenses (TRCP 94) — plead only with a factual basis

- **Limitations**: 4 years (Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 16.004; subsection (c)
  covers open and stated accounts). Accrual generally at default. Partial payment
  does not revive a time-barred consumer debt, and a debt buyer may not sue or
  threaten suit on one (Tex. Fin. Code § 392.307).
- **Payment / accord and satisfaction**: prior settlement with the original creditor
  or a prior buyer; ask for cancelled checks, 1099-C.
- **Mistaken identity / identity theft**: wrong-person suits are common in volume
  filing. If ID theft, also trigger FCRA disputes and a police report.
- **Release, waiver, estoppel**: prior "settled in full" letters.
- Do NOT plead boilerplate defenses without facts — it reads as noise and invites
  discovery about defenses that don't exist.

## Debt-buyer proof problems (why answered cases settle or die)

A debt buyer typically purchases a spreadsheet row plus a "media order" option for
underlying documents that costs them money per account. Once a defendant answers and
requests: (1) the complete chain of title identifying this account in each transfer,
(2) the cardmember agreement in effect at default, (3) a full transaction history from
zero balance, and (4) the identity of a witness competent to sponsor the records —
the plaintiff's expected cost exceeds the expected recovery on most sub-$5,000 files.
That is the whole economic engine of this practice line.

## Counterclaims (separate fee tier — Mike must approve before pleading)

- **Texas Debt Collection Act**, Tex. Fin. Code ch. 392: applies to debt collectors
  including debt buyers; prohibits misrepresenting the character, extent, or amount of
  a debt (§ 392.304), threatening suit on time-barred debt (§ 392.307), and more.
  Remedies (§ 392.403): injunction, actual damages, attorney's fees; statutory
  damages for certain violations; a TDCA violation is also a DTPA violation
  (§ 392.404) opening treble-damage exposure. Third-party collectors must hold a
  $10,000 surety bond (§ 392.101) — check the Secretary of State bond registry; suing
  without one is itself a violation.
- **FDCPA**, 15 U.S.C. § 1692 et seq.: 1-year limitations, $1,000 statutory damages
  plus fees; applies to third-party collectors and, generally, debt buyers collecting
  defaulted debt. Common hooks: suing on time-barred debt, misstated amounts,
  contact-after-representation violations.
- Counterclaims flip fee exposure: TDCA/FDCPA fee-shifting makes continued
  prosecution expensive for the plaintiff and funds work beyond the flat fee.

## Settlement posture

Run this analysis before making or responding to any offer:

1. **Collectability**: client's wages are safe (no consumer garnishment in Texas);
   homestead and exempt personal property are safe; exposed assets are basically
   non-exempt bank balances and non-exempt property. A genuinely exempt client has
   little to fear from a judgment — price offers accordingly. Never tell the client
   they are "judgment-proof" without Mike reviewing an asset worksheet.
2. **Proof risk** (above): weaker chain of title → lower offer.
3. **Limitations**: if time-barred, demand dismissal with prejudice; evaluate
   TDCA/FDCPA counterclaim instead of paying anything.
4. **Client goals**: some clients value credit-report cleanup or certainty over
   dollars — settlement terms should include deletion/non-reporting language and
   dismissal **with prejudice**, and must state the payment resolves the account in
   full (watch for 1099-C tax consequences; flag to client, we don't give tax advice).

Anchors (adjust per file; never quote to client as a promise): answered + discovery
pending debt-buyer files commonly resolve for lump sums in the 10–40% range or are
nonsuited; original-creditor suits (Discover, Amex, Capital One in-house) prove up
more easily and settle higher, often on payment plans.

Every settlement must be documented in writing before payment, signed by the
plaintiff's counsel, and include: amount, deadline, dismissal with prejudice, credit
reporting treatment, and mutual release limited to the account.
